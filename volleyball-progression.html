Developmental Progression Analysis

Volleyball Across Grade Bands

How Instructional Design Adapts to Learner Development: K–5 vs. Grades 6–8
Designer: Kevin Terranova, M.S. Grades: K–5 & 6–8 Context: 3 Elementary Schools + Barbour Middle School District: Rockford Public Schools, IL

Why This Analysis Exists

This analysis demonstrates how a single sport—volleyball—requires fundamentally different instructional design depending on learner developmental stage. Both units align to the same Illinois State Standards and SHAPE America frameworks. But every design decision—pacing, equipment, assessment, autonomy—shifts based on where students are physically, cognitively, and socially. This is the core skill of curriculum development: designing not just for content, but for the learner.

Progression Deep Dive

1. Pacing & Skill Introduction

K–5 Elementary

One skill per week, isolated.

  • Week 1: Underhand Serve
  • Week 2: Bumping (Forearm Pass)
  • Week 3: Setting (Overhand Pass)
  • Week 4: Overhand Serve
  • Weeks 5–6: Modified Game Play

Each skill gets a full week. Individual → partner → game within each lesson. Next skill only introduced after the previous one has had a full week of practice.

MS Grades 6–8

One skill per day — 7-day build, then game play.

  • Day 1: Exploration & Serve
  • Day 2: Bump (Forearm Pass)
  • Day 3: Setting
  • Day 4: Serve Receive
  • Day 5: Overhand Serve
  • Day 6: 3-Contact Rally
  • Day 7: Positions & Rotation
  • Days 8–9: Scrimmage & Tournament

Skills build systematically over 7 days. Game play arrives as the culminating experience once students have the full toolkit to actually compete.

Why it changes: Elementary students need a full week per skill to build motor memory before layering complexity. Middle schoolers can absorb a new skill per day because they have stronger working memory and prior physical literacy. The 9-day MS structure still sequences skills deliberately before game play — but at 5x the pace.

2. Equipment Progression

K–5 Elementary

Full equipment ladder. K–2 students often spend entire lessons with balloons.

  • Balloon → beach ball → trainer volleyball → regulation
  • Equipment choice is the primary differentiation lever
  • Same skill, different equipment = differentiated outcome
MS Grades 6–8

Regulation is the default. Volley-lite balls are accommodation, not starting point.

  • Regulation ball from Day 1
  • Volley-lite available for students needing more reaction time
  • Differentiation shifts to task complexity, not equipment
Why it changes: Elementary hand-eye coordination and physical fear of a hard ball requires a graduated equipment approach. By middle school, students have the physical readiness for regulation equipment. Continuing to use modified equipment can limit development. Differentiation instead focuses on tactical complexity and role difficulty.

3. Warm-Up Design

K–5 Elementary

General fitness games. Fun, high-energy, not volleyball-specific.

  • Tic Tac Toe relay races
  • RPS Showdown (hoop jumps)
  • Sharks & Minnows
  • Walk/Jog intervals

Priority: get students moving, build cardiovascular fitness, create positive energy for the lesson ahead.

MS Grades 6–8

Sport-specific dynamic conditioning. Mirrors actual volleyball movement.

  • High knees, butt kickers, karaoke, back pedal, jog/sprint
  • Creative tag games: Yoshi Run, Freeze Tag, Poly Spot Knockout
  • Permanent circuit — students run it on entry without instruction

Priority: prepare the body for volleyball-specific demands, build athletic movement vocabulary, maintain engagement through variety.

Why it changes: Elementary warm-ups prioritize general fitness and buy-in — students need to be excited before focused instruction. Middle school warm-ups are designed with sport transfer in mind. Karaoke footwork, back pedaling, and reactive sprints all appear in real volleyball situations. The warm-up is part of the curriculum, not just a pre-lesson ritual.

4. Game Play Integration

K–5 Elementary

Skills first, game play last. Modified game only after all individual skills are taught.

  • Game play reserved for final 1–2 weeks
  • K–2: cooperative “keep it up” format
  • 3–5: modified games with full rotation and scoring
  • Teacher manages all game logistics
MS Grades 6–8

Skills built systematically, game play as culmination.

  • Days 1–7: Structured skill development, no live game
  • Day 8: Full 3v3 Scrimmage with teacher coaching stops
  • Day 9: Round-robin Tournament, self-officiated
  • All 7 skills apply simultaneously in live competition
Why it changes: Both units build skills before game play — but the MS game is far more formalized. The tournament on Day 9 has real stakes: competitive scoring, rotation rules, and self-officiation. The difference isn’t philosophy, it’s scope. The K-5 game is cooperative and teacher-managed; the MS game is competitive and student-run. That shift in complexity and ownership is the entire point.

5. Student Autonomy & Role

K–5 Elementary

Teacher-directed throughout.

  • Teacher demonstrates, sets up drills, manages games
  • Students follow structured sequences with clear teacher guidance
  • Peer feedback introduced gradually (partner counting, encouragement)
  • Closure is teacher-led Q&A
MS Grades 6–8

Structured independence throughout, full autonomy at tournament.

  • Days 1–7: Permanent warm-up circuit run independently on entry
  • Day 7: Students manage their own positioning and rotation
  • Day 8: Student-led team debrief after scrimmage
  • Day 9: Self-officiated tournament — “Call it on yourself first”
Why it changes: Elementary students need structure and teacher presence to feel safe and learn effectively. Middle schoolers need ownership to stay engaged — too much teacher direction feels infantilizing. Giving them real responsibility (self-officiating, rotation management, team debrief) deepens rule comprehension, builds leadership, and increases buy-in. The teacher shifts from director to facilitator.

6. Assessment Strategy

K–5 Elementary

Cue-based observation. Specific, measurable, form-focused.

  • “Demonstrate 2 of 4 cues for the underhand serve”
  • “Bump the ball into the hoop from the poly spot”
  • Teacher observes against explicit cue checklist
  • Self-assessment and peer feedback introduced in game weeks
MS Grades 6–8

Game performance & tactical application.

  • Can the student apply skills in a live scrimmage or tournament?
  • Are they reading the play and choosing the right skill?
  • Are they communicating with teammates before the ball arrives?
  • Rubric scored primarily on Days 8–9; self-assessment closes the unit
Why it changes: Elementary assessment targets acquisition — can they DO it? Middle school assessment targets transfer — can they USE it when it counts? This mirrors Bloom’s Taxonomy: elementary students work at knowledge and application levels, while middle school pushes into analysis (reading the game) and synthesis (making tactical decisions in real time).

Theoretical Framework

Both units draw from established learning theory. The emphasis shifts as the learner develops.

K–5 Focus

Vygotsky’s Zone of Proximal Development

Scaffolding from individual → partner → group → game mirrors the ZPD progression. Equipment modification (balloons → regulation) is a concrete scaffolding mechanism. Support is gradually removed as competence grows.

MS Focus

Teaching Games for Understanding (TGfU)

Skills are built in deliberate sequence, then applied in a competitive game. The scrimmage and tournament give students tactical context — they understand why each skill matters when they need it under pressure.

Both Units

Self-Determination Theory

Both units build competence (skill mastery), autonomy (increasing choice and ownership), and relatedness (teamwork, peer interaction). The MS unit pushes autonomy harder; the K-5 unit emphasizes competence through structured success.
