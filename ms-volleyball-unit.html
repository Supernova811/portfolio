MIDDLE SCHOOL VOLLEYBALL
DAY 1 OF 9
Volleyball Exploration & Underhand Serve Introduction
Learning Targets (SWBAT)
• I can execute an underhand serve using a step, swing, and contact at the heel of my hand
• I can explain the purpose of a serve in volleyball
• I can identify at least 2 things I want to improve after watching my classmates
Equipment
• Volleyballs (1 per student)
• Net or tape line across the gym
• Cones to mark personal space / target zones
Standards
19.A19.B21.A
S1S4
Essential Question
What is the purpose of a serve — and where on your hand should the ball make contact for the most control?  |  What did you already know about volleyball before today?
Warm-Up — Experimental Volleyball  (5–8 min)

Every student grabs a volleyball. Music on. No instructions — just go. Students have full freedom to toss and bump, set to themselves, try to serve over the net, or play catch with a partner. Teacher does NOT coach during this time.

Teacher's job: Walk and observe. Who already has a natural bump form? Who uses their fists vs. a platform? Who can get a ball over the net and who can't? Who immediately goes for overhand vs. staying underhand? This is your baseline read before any instruction begins.

When music stops: "Nobody taught you anything yet — but I already learned a lot about what you can do. Today we start building on what you already have."

This is the only day where you observe without correcting. Let them show you who they are first. Every coaching decision for Days 2–9 starts right here.

Lesson Focus — Underhand Serve  (20–25 min)

Teach each cue one at a time — demo, then students practice before the next:

Step & Swing — Opposite foot steps forward as your hitting arm swings back, then forward. If you're right-handed: left foot steps, right arm swings. Timing = everything. Have students dry-swing without the ball first.
Heel of the Hand — Contact happens with the heel (fleshy bottom) of your open hand — NOT your fist, NOT your palm. Make a flat hand, feel the heel pad. "That's your contact point. Hit with that every time."
Hold the Ball Still — Your non-hitting hand holds the ball in front of you at waist level. Don't toss it — hold it steady and swing through it. "One hand holds, one hand hits."
Follow Through High — After contact, your hitting arm continues up toward the target. Low follow-through = ball goes into the net. Follow through toward where you want it to go.

Serving Practice Progression:

① Students practice the swing motion without a ball — 10 reps, teacher coaches arm path. ② Add the ball: contact at the heel from 5 feet from the net. ③ Back up to the 10-foot line. ④ Back up to the end line — serve over the net. Count made serves out of 5 attempts.

Coaching focus: most students will hit with their fist or slap with their palm. Constantly redirect to the heel pad. Have students tap the heel of their hand and say "this is my contact point" before each serve.

Game — Serve & Score  (10–12 min)

Set up 4–5 target zones on the opposite side of the net using cones (back corners = 3 pts, mid-court = 2 pts, front zone = 1 pt). Students line up to serve from the end line. Each student gets 3 serves and tracks their score.

Run 2–3 rounds. Students challenge themselves to match or beat their previous round score. Teacher calls out good form publicly — "Did you see that follow-through? That's exactly it." No score-shaming. Focus is on form + getting it over, not just point total.

Closure & Assessment  (3–5 min)
Closure
Students sit at the end line. "Give me the 4 cues for an underhand serve — say them to your neighbor." "Why do we step with the opposite foot?" "What's the most common mistake you saw today?" Preview Day 2: the ball doesn't always come at you at shoulder height — tomorrow we learn how to handle a low ball without letting it hit the ground.
Assessment
Observation during practice: are students contacting with the heel of the hand? Does the follow-through go high or stay low? Serve & Score results: how many students can clear the net consistently from the end line? Note students who will need extra support on Day 4 (serve receive).
Differentiation & Teacher Notes
Modify down: Move students closer to the net (10–15 ft). Use a beach ball or softer volleyball for students with poor hand-eye coordination. Allow a toss (hold ball lower, contact higher) to reduce timing demands.  Modify up: Challenge advanced students to hit specific target zones — left corner only, then right corner only. Start working on consistent placement, not just clearing the net.
Note: Day 1 is your diagnostic. Notice who has experience, who is afraid of the ball, and who has the athleticism but not the mechanics. Use these observations to set up deliberate partnerships for Day 2's partner drills. Experimental Volleyball also tells you which students can self-manage and which ones need structure.
MIDDLE SCHOOL VOLLEYBALL
DAY 2 OF 9
Forearm Pass (Bump)
Learning Targets (SWBAT)
• I can demonstrate a forearm pass using a flat platform below my wrists
• I can direct a forearm pass to a partner consistently
• I can explain why arm angle determines where the ball goes
Equipment
• Volleyballs (1 per pair)
• Net
• Cones for spacing
Standards
19.A19.B21.B
S1S2
Essential Question
Why does the angle of your arms change where the ball goes?  |  What does it mean to "give" with the ball — and why does that make the pass more accurate?
Warm-Up — Volleyball Touch Circuit  (5–8 min)

This is the permanent warm-up for Days 2–9. Students run through it without being told by Day 4. Build the habit now.

① Arm circles forward 10 sec, backward 10 sec  ② Wrist circles + shoulder rolls (10 sec)  ③ Self-toss + catch with forearm platform position — no actual bump yet, just the shape (10 reps)  ④ Self-toss + controlled forearm bump to self (10 reps)  ⑤ Self-toss + set to self (10 reps)  ⑥ Three controlled underhand serves at the wall or net

Coaching today: "Check your platform — are your arms flat? Wrists together? That's what we're practicing all of today."

Warm-Up Game — Sharks & Minnows  (4–5 min)

Minnows each have a volleyball and must travel across the gym while controlling their ball (self-bumping or setting to themselves). Sharks try to knock the ball away with an open hand (a legal volleyball strike — no grabbing). If your ball touches the ground, you become a shark.

Start with 3–4 sharks. Last minnow standing wins the round. Run 2 rounds. This builds ball control under pressure — students are self-bumping while avoiding defenders, which is exactly what serve receive demands.

Coaching: "Keep the platform up — if you close your fists or hug the ball, you lose control and you'll lose it to a shark faster."

Lesson Focus — Forearm Pass (Bump)  (20–25 min)

Teach the platform first — before the ball ever arrives:

Flat Platform — Clasp your hands together (one fist, one wraps around). Press your thumbs flat and side-by-side — NO thumbs pointing up. Your forearms should form a flat, unified surface from wrist to elbow. "Make a shelf. The ball rolls off a shelf, not a hill."
Contact Below the Wrist — The ball contacts your forearms between your wrists and elbows — NOT your wrists, NOT your hands. If it hits your wrists, it bounces sideways. Have students point to the contact zone on their arm before every pass.
Square Your Shoulders — Before the ball arrives, turn your body so your shoulders face where you want the ball to go. You can't aim the platform if your shoulders are sideways. "Shoulders to your target, every time."
Push, Don't Swing — Arms absorb the ball, then push up and forward. No big arm swing — that sends the ball flying everywhere. Legs drive the power. "Legs up, arms steady."

Partner Passing Drill:

① Coach tosses to student — student bumps back to coach (teacher or aide), 10 reps each. ② Partner pairs: one tosses underhand, other bumps back. Count consecutive passes without the ball hitting the ground. ③ Increase distance slightly: 12–15 feet apart. Focus on platform angle — if ball goes left, your arms angled left. Track the highest consecutive count each pair reaches.

Game — Bump & Keep It Up  (10–12 min)

Groups of 3–4. Students stand in a small circle and try to keep the volleyball in the air using only forearm passes. No catches, no sets — bumps only. Count consecutive touches without the ball hitting the floor. Each group tracks their best streak.

Round 1: Catches allowed to reset (build confidence).  Round 2: No catches — bump only.  Round 3: Must call "mine!" before every touch.

Best group streak gets recognized publicly at closure — but emphasize communication and hustle over raw numbers.

Closure & Assessment  (3–5 min)
Closure
Students hold their platform position in front of them. "Show me — thumbs flat? Arms flat? Wrists together?" Check and correct from a distance. "Why do your shoulders need to face the target before the ball gets there?" Preview Day 3: once you can get the ball up, someone needs to put it in position for a hit — that's the setter's job. Tomorrow we learn to set.
Assessment
Platform check: are thumbs flat? Are students swinging their arms or using their legs? Partner drill counts: which pairs are above 5 consecutive? Above 10? Keep it Up: note communication — are students calling the ball or just running into each other? This tells you who's ready for 3-contact sequences (Day 6).
Differentiation & Teacher Notes
Modify down: Start with balloon or beach ball to slow ball speed. Allow the student to catch the toss, reset platform, then bump. Focus on platform shape over movement.  Modify up: Partner passes off a real serve (not a toss). Move farther apart. Count consecutive passes from a live underhand serve.
Note: The most common error is students clasping hands with thumbs up or wrapping fingers together unevenly — the platform goes uneven and the ball sprays sideways. Stand where you can see arms from the side. Flat arms are invisible from behind. Sharks & Minnows doubles as a buy-in activity — students who struggled to connect today will approach the ball with much less hesitation by the next time you play it.

DAY 3 OF 9
Setting
Learning Targets (SWBAT)
• I can set a volleyball using my finger pads with hands above my forehead
• I can direct a set to a specific target location
• I can explain the setter's role in a volleyball rally
Equipment
• Volleyballs (1 per 2–3 students)
• Net
• Cones for target zones
Standards
19.A19.B21.B
S1S2
Essential Question
What makes a good set "catchable" for a hitter?  |  Why does the ball need to briefly rest against your hands instead of being slapped or caught?
Warm-Up — Volleyball Touch Circuit  (5–8 min)

Students run the permanent circuit on entry. Hold students accountable — if they're standing around waiting, they're doing it wrong. Coach today: "On station 5 — set to yourself. Push through your fingertips all the way to the top. That's exactly what today is about."

Transition Game — Tick Tick Boom  (4–5 min)

Students form a large circle. One volleyball is passed around using forearm passes only — no setting yet (that's today's lesson). One student stands in the middle and spins a cone on the floor. While the cone is spinning, everyone chants "Tick… Tick… Tick…" — the moment the cone falls, the whole class yells "BOOM!"

Whoever is holding the ball when BOOM happens does a quick consequence (10 squats, a short lap, 5 push-ups) and immediately rejoins the circle. Middle player steps back after spinning — no controlling when the cone falls. Rotate the middle player after every 2–3 booms.

This is a perfect forearm pass review from Day 2 — passing fast under pressure, platform up, eyes on the ball, nobody wants to be caught holding it when the cone drops.

Coaching: "Platform — not fists! Call the pass out loud before you send it."

Lesson Focus — Setting  (18–22 min)

Demo the hand position before any ball contact:

Make the Windows — Bring both hands above your forehead, fingertips touching, thumbs and index fingers forming a triangle. Look through that triangle — that's where the ball will sit. "If you can see a tiny window through your hands, you've got it."
Ball Rests, Doesn't Slap — The ball briefly rests on all 10 fingertips — NOT the palms, NOT the wrists. It should feel like the ball is resting in a nest for a split second before you push it out. If it sounds like a slap, you're catching it wrong.
Extend and Follow — Push through the ball by fully extending your arms in the direction of your target. Your fingers should finish pointing at where you want the ball to go. "Arms go where the ball goes."
Eyes on the Target — Look at where you're sending the ball, not at the ball itself. If you're watching your hands, you're missing your target. Find the spot. Set there.

Practice Progression:

① Students hold a ball and press it overhead — no toss yet. Check: is it fingertips or palms? ② Self-toss low (2 feet), set to self — count 5 in a row without the ball touching the floor or palms. ③ Partners: one tosses, one sets back. ④ Partner sets — receiver catches, resets, sets back. Count consecutive clean sets. ⑤ Advanced: move to set over net to partner.

Game — Set & Score  (10–12 min)

Groups of 3. Tosser → Setter → Target Zone. Tosser (same player each round) tosses the ball to the setter. Setter attempts to set the ball into a target zone (cones) on the other side of the net or across the floor. Points scored for landing in the zone.

Rotate roles after every 3 sets. Focus: setter must call the target out loud before setting — "LEFT ZONE!" — builds the habit of communicating direction before contact. Team with the most points after 3 full rotations wins the round. Play 2 rounds.

Closure & Assessment  (3–5 min)
Closure
Students hold "the windows" hand position in front of their face. Check from a distance — are fingertips touching? Thumbs and index fingers forming the triangle? "What's the setter's job in a rally?" (Answer: give the hitter a good ball in a good spot.) Preview Day 4: now that you can get the ball up (bump) and put it in position (set), we put those two together — starting with serve receive.
Assessment
Watch for palms vs. fingertips — the most common error. Listen for slapping sounds during practice (palm contact). Partner sets: who can maintain consecutive sets above 5? Note students who push out vs. extend through — extension is the marker of a quality set. Tick Tick Boom: was bumping from Day 2 improving?
Differentiation & Teacher Notes
Modify down: Allow two-handed catch and release (focus on hand position and extension only — take ball control out of the equation). Use a beach ball — slower speed means more time to form the hand position.  Modify up: Back sets — set without looking at the target (behind the body). Quick sets — short, low, fast. Push them toward game-level speed.
Note: Setting is the hardest skill in this unit to learn from scratch. Expect 30–40% of students to struggle with fingertip-only contact all week. The "windows" cue is the one that clicks fastest because it gives them something physical to check before the ball arrives. Do not rush past setting — Day 6 (3-touch rally) depends on a functional setter.

Serve Receive — Bumping the Serve
Learning Targets (SWBAT)
• I can position my body to receive an underhand serve before it arrives
• I can direct a serve receive bump toward a designated setter target
• I can communicate "mine!" before contacting the ball
Equipment
• Volleyballs (1 per group of 3)
• Net
• Cones for setter target zone
Standards
19.A19.B21.B
S1S2
Essential Question
How do you decide WHERE on the court to be before the serve is even hit?  |  Why does calling "mine!" before you touch the ball make your whole team better?
Warm-Up — Volleyball Touch Circuit  (5–8 min)

Students run the permanent circuit on entry. Students should be starting this independently by now — if they're not, use this as a teachable moment about routine. Coach the circuit with an eye toward serve receive: "On station 4 — I want your platform to aim toward the center of the gym. That's your target when you receive today."

Warm-Up Game — Sharks & Minnows  (4–5 min)

Same as Day 2. Minnows self-bump or set their ball while crossing the gym. Sharks knock balls away. New rule today: Minnows must call "mine!" before every self-contact. If a shark hears you call it — you're safe for 2 seconds. This builds the verbal habit that carries into serve receive.

Start with 4 sharks. Last 3 minnows win. Run 2 rounds.

Lesson Focus — Serve Receive  (20–25 min)

The serve receive is the most important skill in volleyball — if you can't get the serve up, the rally never starts.

Read Early — Watch the server's hitting arm before the ball is even released. The arm tells you: where is it going? Direction, speed, and height all start at the server's shoulder. Move your feet BEFORE the ball crosses the net — not after.
Move First, Platform Second — Get behind the ball, then form the platform. If you're reaching for the ball sideways with an already-formed platform, you've already lost. Feet move, then arms lock in.
Cushion the Ball — Absorb the ball by slightly lowering your platform as it arrives (like catching a drop). Your legs give slightly — don't tense up and smash it. "Cushion, then push."
Call It! — Before you contact, say "mine!" loud enough for every teammate to hear. Nobody else moves. You own it.

Serve-Bump-Catch Drill:

Groups of 3. Server (end line) → Receiver (mid-court) → Catcher at setter target (cone zone). Server sends an underhand serve. Receiver reads, moves, calls "mine!" and bumps to the catcher. Catcher gives verbal feedback: "good platform," "too far left," "excellent!" Rotate every 5 serves. Count successful passes to the target zone per rotation.

Game — Serve-Bump-Set-Catch  (10–12 min)

Extend the Serve-Bump-Catch drill by adding a setter. Groups of 4. Server → Receiver bumps → Setter sets → Catcher. Count successful completions of the full sequence without the ball hitting the floor. Each team tries to beat their own record.

Constraint: Setter must call "set!" after the receiver bumps. Receiver must call "mine!" on the serve. Two verbal calls required for a successful point. This is the foundation of real volleyball — serve receive → set → attack. The chains start here.

Run for 8–10 minutes. Rotate roles every 4 successful completions.

Closure & Assessment  (3–5 min)
Closure
"What does 'read early' mean?" (Watch the server's arm before the ball is released.) "What's the first thing you do when you see where the serve is going?" (Move your feet.) "Why does calling 'mine!' make your team better?" (Prevents collisions, builds trust, everyone knows who's taking it.) Preview Day 5: most of you are serving underhand. Tomorrow we add power — overhand serve.
Assessment
Observation: are students moving their feet early or waiting for the ball to arrive before reacting? Are "mine!" calls happening consistently? Drill count: what percentage of serve receive attempts were directed to the target zone? Note students who freeze at the net — they're not reading the serve and need extra coaching before Day 7 (positions).
Differentiation & Teacher Notes
Modify down: Have the "server" toss instead of serve — reduces speed and unpredictability so students can focus on platform mechanics only. Bring the target zone closer.  Modify up: Server uses a real game serve. Add a second receiver — they must communicate who takes the ball before calling "mine!"
Note: Students who did well in Day 2 (bumping) often struggle here because serve receive is moving + reacting + directing — three things at once. The "Read Early" cue is the key unlock: the moment students start watching the arm instead of the ball, everything else gets easier. Give it a full 3 minutes of focused drill before moving on.

DAY 5 OF 9
Overhand Serve
Learning Targets (SWBAT)
• I can execute an overhand serve with a controlled ball toss and heel-of-hand contact above my shoulder
• I can consistently serve over the net from at least mid-court
• I can explain the tactical advantage of an overhand serve over an underhand serve
Equipment
• Volleyballs (1 per student)
• Net
• Cones for target zones and distance markers
Standards
19.A19.B21.A
S1S4
Essential Question
How does an overhand serve give you more power and control than underhand?  |  Why is a consistent ball toss the most important part of the overhand serve?
Warm-Up — Volleyball Touch Circuit  (5–8 min)

Permanent circuit. Add: during the underhand serve station, students practice the toss only — no swing, just toss and catch. Right-handed toss = left hand releases straight up about 12–18 inches above the head. "Same spot every time. That's the whole game." Coach the toss before the lesson begins.

Transition Game — Tick Tick Boom  (4–5 min)

Circle. Ball passes using sets or bumps — student chooses. Cone in the middle. "Tick… Tick… BOOM!" — whoever holds the ball does a consequence. New rule: if you use a set, you must call "set!" first. If you use a bump, call "bump!" Verbal call before every contact or the pass doesn't count and you redo it.

Rotate the cone-spinner every 3 booms. Keep energy high — pace is everything in this game.

Lesson Focus — Overhand Serve  (20–25 min)

The overhand serve is built on one thing: the toss. Get the toss right and everything else follows.

Toss to Your Number 1 — Imagine a clock above your head. Toss the ball to 12 o'clock — directly above your hitting shoulder, about 12–18 inches up. Not too far forward, not behind your head. Toss it to the same spot every single time. "No toss — no serve."
High Five the Ball — At the peak of the toss, reach up and slightly forward and strike the ball with an open hand — heel of the hand first, like a high five above your head. Arm is fully extended at contact. "Reach high, then snap through."
Snap Your Wrist — At contact, snap your wrist forward (fingers follow through over the ball). This creates topspin, which brings the ball down into the court instead of sailing out the back. No wrist snap = floater or out of bounds.
Step and Reach — Opposite foot steps forward as the toss goes up. Weight transfers forward through the serve. If your feet stay still, you lose all your power.

Practice Progression:

① Toss only: students release 10 tosses each, ball must go straight up and land on the hitting shoulder side — no catches, just watch where it drops. ② Add contact: short distance (10 ft) serving against the wall. ③ Move to net: overhand serve from 15 feet. ④ Move to full service line. Students who cannot clear the net may remain at mid-court.

Game — Serve Wars  (10–12 min)

Divide the class into two teams — one side serves underhand, the other serves overhand. Three servers from each team serve simultaneously at the opposing team's target cones. First team to knock all 5 cones to the ground wins the round. Reset and go again — switch serving styles.

After 3 rounds: "Which serve put more balls in the target zone?" Debrief: overhand generates more power and direction options — but underhand is more consistent when you're still learning. Both are legal. Use what you can control. "A serve in the court beats a bomb out of bounds every time."

Closure & Assessment  (3–5 min)
Closure
"What's the most important part of the overhand serve?" (The toss.) "If your serve keeps going into the net — what's the fix?" (Higher toss, higher contact point.) "Which serve are you choosing to use on Day 9 — and why?" Preview Day 6: you can serve, receive, and set. Now it's time to put all three together in one sequence — that's real volleyball.
Assessment
Toss consistency — watch for students who have wildly inconsistent tosses (ball moves forward or backward). These students will struggle to build the overhand serve without extra toss-only work. Track who successfully crosses the net overhand from the full service line. Note these students — they're your early leaders for Day 9 tournament.
Differentiation & Teacher Notes
Modify down: Allow the student to stand at mid-court. Focus on the toss only — do not force overhand if the student cannot yet control underhand. Underhand from end line is still a passing grade.  Modify up: Float serve — barely snap the wrist, let the ball float with no spin. Harder to receive. Jump serve — toss forward and approach. Only for students who have full overhand control.
Note: The overhand serve is where student confidence diverges sharply. Students who come in with baseball experience pick it up in 5 minutes. Students without throwing backgrounds may take all 9 days. The toss is the single biggest intervention point — a consistent toss fixes 70% of overhand serve problems.

DAY 6 OF 9
3-Contact Rally — Bump, Set, Attack
Learning Targets (SWBAT)
• I can execute a 3-contact sequence (bump → set → attack) as part of a team
• I can communicate with teammates before each contact
• I can explain why 3 contacts is more effective than 1 or 2
Equipment
• Volleyballs (1 per group of 3)
• Net (lowered if needed)
• Cones for target zones
Standards
19.A19.C21.B
S1S2
Essential Question
Why is a 3-contact rally more powerful than sending it over on the first touch?  |  What does the setter need from the first contact to do their job well?
Warm-Up — Volleyball Touch Circuit  (5–8 min)

Permanent circuit. Coaching cue today: during partner bump station, students must alternate contacts — one bump each, back and forth. No catching between. This is the pattern that drives today's 3-touch drill. "Control your first touch. That's what makes the second touch possible."

Warm-Up Game — Sharks & Minnows  (4–5 min)

Same as Days 2 and 4. Minnows self-bump or set across the gym. Sharks knock balls down. New rule: Minnows must alternate bump, set, bump, set — cannot use the same contact twice in a row. This forces students to set at unpredictable heights, exactly what Day 6's drill requires.

Lesson Focus — 3-Contact Sequence  (20–25 min)

Teach the sequence before touching a ball. Use three students as visual demonstrations:

Three colored cones: Green = first contact (bump the serve)  →  Yellow = second contact (set it up)  →  Red = third contact (attack/push it over). These three roles are always in sequence — no skipping.

Bump It Up (Green) — First contact gets the ball up and toward the center. Aim high — better to send it too high than too flat. The setter needs time. "Bump it up and call 'SET!'"
Set It High (Yellow) — Second contact is the setup. Setter calls "set!" moves to the ball, and puts it 2–3 feet off the net, high enough for the attacker to approach. "Give the hitter a good ball — they need height and location."
Attack at the Peak (Red) — Third contact goes over. Aim deep into the court (back third). Hit at the highest point of the ball's arc — not on the way down. Even a controlled push or tip counts as an attack. "Deep ball is hard to dig. Short ball is easy. Go deep."
Talk Through the Whole Thing — All three players call their role out loud: "BUMP!" → "SET!" → "HIT!" If you're not talking, your team is guessing. Guessing causes collisions.

3-Touch Drill: Groups of 3. One ball. Serve is thrown in. Green bumps to Yellow. Yellow sets to Red. Red attacks over net (or at a target). Count successful 3-touch sequences per 10 attempts. Teacher calls out each role: "Who's Green? Where's Yellow standing?"

Game — 3-Touch Challenge  (10–12 min)

3v3 over the net. Same rule as in competitive volleyball: each team must use all 3 contacts before sending the ball over. If a team sends it over on 1 or 2 touches — no point. Ball must go bump → set → attack (or any controlled 3rd touch) to earn a point.

First team to 7 points wins the set. Play 2 sets. Teacher pauses play after notable sequences to highlight good communication or decision-making. This is the first full volleyball game of the unit — students who struggled with individual skills often surprise you when there's a real game with a score.

Closure & Assessment  (3–5 min)
Closure
"What's the setter's job — and what does the setter need from the first contact to do it?" (High ball, toward center.) "What happens to your team if the second contact is too low or too far from the net?" (Hitter can't swing, no good attack.) "What verbal call goes with each contact?" Preview Day 7: now that you can run the 3-touch sequence — where does each person start? Tomorrow is positions and rotation.
Assessment
3-Touch Challenge: what percentage of rallies used all 3 contacts? Are players communicating verbally or just running to the ball? Note: students who are strong at one role (only bumpers, only setters) — by Day 8 they need to rotate through all three. Teacher observation key today: who tracks ball path vs. who freezes waiting for the ball to come to them.
Differentiation & Teacher Notes
Modify down: Allow a catch after the bump so the setter has time to get in position. Count 2-contact sequences as success if 3 is too hard.  Modify up: No catches ever. Require the attacker to call their target zone before swinging. Play with a real overhand serve to start each rally.
Note: The biggest teaching moment on Day 6 is showing students WHY 3 contacts beats 1. Ask: "If you send the ball over on the first touch from mid-court — where's it going? Can your team aim it?" The answer is almost always no. Three contacts lets you aim. That's the sell. Once they understand the WHY, they buy into the process even if it's harder.

Court Positions & Rotation
Learning Targets (SWBAT)
• I can identify and name the 6 court positions in volleyball
• I can demonstrate correct rotation order after my team wins a sideout
• I can explain WHY teams rotate — and what strategic advantage it creates
Equipment
• Volleyballs
• Net
• Numbered position cards or cones (1–6)
Standards
19.C20.A21.B
S2S5
Essential Question
Why do teams rotate in volleyball — and what's the strategic advantage of spreading your best players through every position?  |  What does "know your zone" mean, and how does it prevent collisions?
Warm-Up — Volleyball Touch Circuit  (5–8 min)

Permanent circuit. Coaching cue today: label each student a position number (1–6) before the circuit begins. After the circuit ends, tell them that their position number tells them exactly where to stand and what to do when the ball comes over the net. "Everything today connects to where you are on the court."

Transition Game — Tick Tick Boom  (4–5 min)

Circle game. Ball passed using bumps or sets. Cone in the middle. "Tick… Tick… BOOM!" — consequence for whoever holds it. New challenge: when the teacher calls a position number (1–6) mid-game, players who know they're in that position must take two extra turns passing before sending the ball on. Builds position awareness in a low-stakes context.

Lesson Focus — Positions & Rotation  (18–22 min)

Draw the 6-position map on the board or floor:

Front row (net side): 4 (Left Front) · 3 (Middle Front) · 2 (Right Front)  |  Back row: 5 (Left Back) · 6 (Middle Back) · 1 (Right Back / Server)

Know Your Zone — Each player is responsible for a section of the court. If the ball is in your zone, it's yours. If it's between two zones, you call it. You do NOT run to balls in someone else's zone — that's how collisions happen and balls hit the floor uncovered.
Call "Mine!" Before You Touch It — Every contact must be claimed verbally. No exceptions. If two players both go without calling it — it's nobody's fault and everybody's problem.
Rotation Is Clockwise — When your team wins a sideout (wins the point while receiving), everyone rotates one position clockwise. Position 1 becomes Position 6, Position 6 becomes Position 5, and so on. The new Position 1 serves.
No Ball Hog — Trust your teammates. If a ball comes to Position 6, the Position 6 player takes it — even if Position 5 is faster. Zones are assigned, not earned by speed.

Walk-Through Drill: Put 6 students on one side of the court. Teacher calls a position number — those students raise their hand and point to their zone. Then teacher calls "rotate!" and students move clockwise to the next position. Run 3 full rotations. Then: teacher throws a ball to a zone — which number player calls "mine!" and takes it?

Rotation Game: 3v3 or 4v4, short side of the net. Teams rotate every 3 points — whether they win or lose. Focus: every rotation, players point to their position before the serve happens. Teacher stops play after every rotation to confirm everyone is in the right spot.

Game — Rotation Rally  (10–12 min)

3v3 (or 4v4 adapted). Full rotation rules apply — rotate clockwise after sideout, new server takes Position 1. Each server gets one rotation to serve as many points as possible, then the team rotates. Teams track how many total points they scored in each rotation. Which rotation generated the most points for your team? That's your strongest player combination — and that's real volleyball strategy.

Teacher announces: "Rotate!" after every 3 points regardless of who scored — this keeps the game moving and gives everyone a chance to serve.

Closure & Assessment  (3–5 min)
Closure
Students stand in a row of 6 on the end line. "Point to Position 4." "Point to Position 1." "If Position 1 scores the point while receiving — what happens?" (Rotate clockwise.) "Who rotates to serve?" (New Position 1.) Preview Day 8: tomorrow you're putting everything together in a scrimmage — serve, receive, set, attack, and rotate.
Assessment
Walk-through drill: did students know their zone immediately, or did they need to look around? Rotation Game: are rotations happening smoothly, or is every rotation a 2-minute argument? Note students who default to chasing all balls — they're not trusting the zone system. These are the students to coach hardest on Day 8 for game play.
Differentiation & Teacher Notes
Modify down: Limit to 3 positions instead of 6 (Left, Middle, Right). Run a simpler back-and-forth rotation — when your team scores, rotate. Keep it to one rule at a time.  Modify up: Add a serving strategy challenge: serve to specific zones (deep left, short middle). Setter always moves to Position 2 after the serve — build the positional pattern.
Note: Rotation is the rule students complain about most and understand least at first. The key is making it feel purposeful: "Rotation means everyone serves. Everyone serves means you can't hide." That framing turns rotation from a confusing rule into a fairness principle — which MS students actually care about. Pre-assign teams for the Day 9 tournament tonight so you're not losing 10 minutes of class to team drama tomorrow.

Essential Question
What does your team do well right now?  |  If you could go home and practice one volleyball skill tonight, what would it be — and why?
Warm-Up — Volleyball Touch Circuit  (5–8 min)

Permanent circuit. Students run it on entry without instruction. Today: after the circuit, teacher announces the day's scrimmage teams and game format. Students know their team before the first whistle.

Warm-Up Game — Sharks & Minnows  (4–5 min)

Final run of Sharks & Minnows. Minnows self-bump and set while crossing. Today's rule: minnows can protect themselves by quickly setting the ball above their head — while the ball is in the air above them, sharks cannot knock it. Forces a controlled set under pressure. Run 2 quick rounds.

Lesson Focus — Scrimmage with Coaching Stops  (25–28 min)

Pre-scrimmage skill review (3 min max): Teacher calls 5 cues — one per skill. Students repeat back. "Platform flat?" "Windows above the forehead?" "Toss to 12 o'clock?" "3 contacts before it goes over?" "Call mine before every touch?" This is the mental checklist they carry into the game.

3v3 Scrimmage — full volleyball rules:

Teams of 3. Standard scoring — rally scoring (point on every serve). Rotate after sideout. 3 contacts required before sending the ball over (bump → set → attack). Serves from the end line — player's choice of serve type.

Games to 7, win by 2. Switch opponents after each game. Teacher circulates — stop play for individual teaching moments only (not constantly). Call your coaching stops around decisions, not missed hits:

Talk Early — "I stopped the game because nobody called the serve. Call it the moment you see where it's going — not after it drops."
Cover the Court — "When your teammate bumps, move to support. If they shanked it, someone needs to be there."
Play the Ball, Not the Player — "Your eyes go to the ball, not to the opponent. Read the ball. The court opens when you stop watching people."

Use coaching stops sparingly — 2 to 3 per game maximum. Let the game breathe. Students learn more from playing than from being stopped.

Closure & Assessment  (5 min)

After last whistle: teams line up for a mandatory handshake line — positive word to each opponent, not just a tap. Then brief team debrief (2 min): "One thing your team communicated well today. One thing you'd fix before tomorrow."

Teacher observation for rubric: this is the primary day for scoring the rubric. Watch for bump form, set form, serve quality, and sportsmanship under game pressure. Note names of students who surprised you — both positively and negatively. These inform tomorrow's tournament bracket and rubric scores.

Preview Day 9: "Tomorrow is the tournament. Everything we've built over 8 days comes out tomorrow. Come ready to compete and come ready to carry yourself like an athlete."

Differentiation & Teacher Notes
Modify down: Allow a catch between contacts — bump, catch, set, catch, attack. This keeps the sequence alive for teams struggling with pace. Remove the 3-contact requirement for teams that can't rally — point is playing, not perfection.  Modify up: Require an overhand serve from the full line. Add a "no bump over" rule — you must set before attacking. Run rally scoring to 11.
Note: Day 8 is your most important teacher observation day. Rubric scores should be filled in before Day 9 when possible. Students who stand out today should be recognized tomorrow — call it in the handshake line, not in front of everyone during the game. Scrimmage teams today should inform your tournament seeding for Day 9 if you care about competitive balance.

Essential Question
What does a great volleyball player look like — in skills AND character?  |  What's one thing you learned about yourself as a competitor during this unit?
Warm-Up — Student Choice  (5–8 min)

Students vote: Sharks & Minnows or Tick Tick Boom for today's warm-up game. Run whichever wins the vote. Volleyball Touch Circuit runs first for 3–4 minutes regardless. Let them own the final day.

After warm-up, post the tournament bracket where students can see it. Teams know their first opponent before any instruction happens.

Tournament Format  (28–32 min)

Mental Checklist (2 min — say this with students before first serve):

Teacher calls each cue — students answer back:

"Forearm pass — what's the key?" → Flat platform, push don't swing.   "Setting — where are your hands?" → Windows above the forehead.   "Serve — what's the most important part?" → The toss.   "Before every contact?" → Call mine!   "What does a good teammate sound like?" → Loud, positive, specific.

Tournament Rules:

3v3 round-robin. 4–5 teams. Games to 7, rally scoring. 3 contacts required (bump → set → attack). Rotation after sideout. Serve from end line — player's choice of type. Self-officiate. Call it on yourself first. No teacher-officiated disputes — teams resolve or the point replays.

Brief 30-second rotation between games — teams move to the next opponent. Post results on bracket after each round. "Today you're not drilling. Today you're playing volleyball."

Teacher observation focus: sportsmanship and communication over raw skill. Note students who call their own infractions, encourage teammates by name, and respond to losing points without drama. Recognize those students publicly at the close.

Unit Reflection  (5 min)

Required reflection before students leave. Students complete the self-assessment form during this time (or prior night if time is tight). At minimum, address one question verbally:

Skill Growth: "What is one skill you improved most during this unit? What did you do differently by Day 9 than you did on Day 1?"
Hardest Part: "What was the hardest volleyball skill for you — and why?"
Transfer: "What skills from this unit can you use in other physical activities — or in your life?"

If time is tight, do it verbally: partner share, then 3–4 share with the class. Don't skip the reflection. Even one answer per student closes the unit with intentionality.

Closure & Assessment  (3–5 min)
Closure + Teacher Close
Handshake line between all teams — positive word to each opponent, not just a tap. Teacher recognizes specific sportsmanship moments observed today.

"Volleyball is 90% communication. The teams that won today weren't always the most skilled — they were the loudest, most organized, most connected. That skill — talking to your team, trusting your teammates, covering for each other — that goes way beyond volleyball. Nice work."
Assessment + Teacher Notes
Tournament — are skills from Days 1–8 showing up in live play? Are students serving successfully, receiving and directing bumps, and completing 3-contact sequences? Sportsmanship — are students completing the handshake genuinely, not performatively?

Finalize rubric scores tonight. Note students who showed the most growth over 9 days — not just the most skilled. Growth is the story worth telling at the next parent conference.
Differentiation & Teacher Notes
Skill balancing: If one team dominates early, add a rule (e.g., all 3 players must touch the ball each rally).  Limited mobility: Allow a permanent setter or "court director" role for students who cannot fully participate in movement.  High-competition students: Address sportsmanship expectations privately before the tournament, not publicly during it.
Note: Pre-assign tournament teams the night before — Day 9 team drama slows everything down. Post the bracket where students see it on entry. The self-assessment closes the unit with intentionality — don't skip it even if short on time. One answer per student beats zero. What students say about their own growth is often the most powerful data point of the whole unit.
